# Editorial Standard

This file defines the standing quality bar for long-form articles. It controls
editorial judgment, not personal voice. Personal voice belongs in `VOICE.md`;
established personal positions belong in `OPINIONS.md`.

## Reader contract

Every article must earn the reader's time.

Prefer:

- argument over topic coverage;
- evidence over adjectives;
- mechanisms over claims;
- concrete examples over abstractions;
- consequences over feature descriptions;
- precision over rhetorical flourish;
- useful tension over artificial balance;
- depth on a few important ideas over shallow completeness.

Do not publish an article whose central observation is merely obvious.

Do not manufacture controversy to compensate for a weak thesis.

Do not create false balance when evidence strongly favors one conclusion.

Do not hide uncertainty.

Do not mistake complexity for sophistication.

## Intellectual standard

A strong article normally has:

- one primary thesis;
- a clear reason the thesis matters to the intended reader;
- 2-5 substantive supporting moves rather than a pile of loosely related points;
- evidence proportional to the strength of its claims;
- an explanation of mechanism where mechanism matters;
- a serious counterargument when one could materially alter the conclusion;
- implications that follow from the reasoning rather than from generic advice.

An article does not need to cover every dimension of a topic. Omission is often
an editorial strength when it creates depth.

## Voice standard

The article should sound written by a person with judgment, not generated by a
system demonstrating completeness.

Natural writing may contain:

- uneven paragraph lengths;
- occasional short paragraphs;
- longer sentences when the reasoning needs qualification;
- selective repetition;
- restrained rhetorical devices;
- honest uncertainty;
- asymmetry in how much space different arguments receive.

Do not manufacture typos, slang, fragments, profanity, anecdotes, or quirks to
make writing appear human.

## Evidence standard

Never invent supporting material.

Use the strongest available sources. Distinguish facts from inference, judgment,
and prediction. Narrow claims when the evidence is weaker than the original
framing.

A precise weakly supported claim is worse than a narrower accurate claim.

## Editing standard

Edit in this order:

1. thesis;
2. evidence;
3. structure;
4. reasoning;
5. specificity;
6. voice and cadence;
7. line-level polish;
8. anti-AI-pattern cleanup.

Do not line-edit a structurally weak article into polished mediocrity.

## Anti-slop standard

AI-like writing is primarily a pattern-density problem, not a forbidden-word
problem. Look for repeated scaffolding, predictable rhythm, fake insight,
synthetic symmetry, generic conclusions, and cliches of thought.

Do not remove a natural rhetorical device merely because models also use it.
Remove it when it is unnecessary, repeated, inflated, or mechanically
predictable.

## Publication decision

The question is not `Is this grammatically correct?`

The questions are:

- Is this true enough to defend?
- Is it interesting enough to publish?
- Does the reasoning survive an informed critic?
- Does the article reward the reader?
- Does the prose sound like authored judgment rather than generated coverage?
